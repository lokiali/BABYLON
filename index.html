The City

A City Between Worlds

Babylon stood on the Euphrates in southern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq.

Its ruins lie about 85 km south of Baghdad, near Hillah in Babil Governorate. Fertile land, river transport and trade routes helped it grow into a political, religious and cultural centre, and the capital of two great Mesopotamian states.

The Gate of Ishtar

The Gate of Ishtar

Built around 575 BC under Nebuchadnezzar II, the gate was a ceremonial entrance to Babylon's inner city, named for the goddess Ishtar.

Its walls were faced with glazed bricks in deep blue and decorated with rows of molded animals: lions, bulls and dragons. Beyond it ran the Processional Way, used for religious processions.

Architecture

Walls, Palaces and Temples

What survives is mostly foundations and brick walls. Each landmark is marked as archaeological remains, partial rebuilding or artistic reconstruction.

01Archaeological remains

The City Walls

Excavations revealed inner and outer defensive walls around the city. Ancient writers gave very different figures for their size, so modern estimates rely on archaeology.

02Remains, partly rebuilt

The Southern Palace

Nebuchadnezzar II's main palace was excavated by the German expedition led by Robert Koldewey (1899–1917). Parts of the site were rebuilt in the 1980s.

03Remains and artistic reconstruction

Etemenanki, the Ziggurat

A stepped temple tower beside the temple of Marduk. Only its foundations survive and its original form is debated. It is often linked with the biblical Tower of Babel.

04Remains, partly rebuilt

The Processional Way

A ceremonial street that led through the Ishtar Gate toward the temple district. Its walls carried molded lions in glazed brick.

Knowledge

Writing, Law and Science

Babylon's scribes wrote on clay. Thousands of tablets let us read the laws, letters, mathematics and astronomy of ancient Mesopotamia.

Cuneiform

Wedge-shaped signs pressed into wet clay with a reed stylus, used for about three thousand years to write Sumerian, Akkadian and other languages.

Law

The Stele of Hammurabi records a long collection of laws in the king's name. It dates to the 18th century BC and is now in the Louvre.

Mathematics

Scribes used a base-60 number system, the origin of our 60-minute hour. The tablet YBC 7289 gives a very close value for the square root of 2.

Astronomy

Babylonian astronomers kept long records of the sky, including the Astronomical Diaries, which run for centuries from the 7th century BC.

Daily Life

Everyday Babylon

Most of what we know comes from contracts, letters and administrative tablets rather than pictures. The scenes below are artistic reconstructions, not photographs.

Farming

Canals from the Euphrates watered fields of barley and groves of date palms.

Trade

Merchants moved goods by river and caravan, and clay contracts recorded loans and sales.

Crafts

Brickmakers, potters, weavers and metalworkers supplied the city.

Faith and festivals

Temples served the gods, with Marduk as the city's patron. The New Year festival, Akitu, was held each spring.

The Legacy

What Remains of Babylon

Today Babylon is an archaeological site near Hillah, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019.

German excavations led by Robert Koldewey (1899–1917) uncovered much of the Neo-Babylonian city. Many finds and related objects are now in museums, including the Ishtar Gate in Berlin, the Stele of Hammurabi in Paris and the Cyrus Cylinder in London.

How we know

Greek and biblical writers described Babylon long after its peak. Excavations and the decipherment of cuneiform in the 19th century added first-hand evidence.

The Hanging Gardens

Greek and Roman authors describe gardens at Babylon, but no Babylonian text mentions them and no garden has been located. Scholars still debate where, or whether, they existed.

Preservation

Parts of the site were rebuilt in the 1980s, and conservation work continues with international partners such as the World Monuments Fund.

Babylon lost its power, but its writing, laws and science travelled far beyond its walls.
